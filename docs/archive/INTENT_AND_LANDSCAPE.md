# Tone Lab interaction research

## The decision the system should help with

**Can I get to a sound I want to play, tell whether it got better, and get back to playing?** The unit of work is a listening decision made in a musical context. A preset is one thing the decision can produce. A loop, a named preference, a rejected direction, and a patch adjusted for a band are useful outcomes too.

For this guitarist, the system spans the instrument, Quad Cortex, computer, playback system, and research tools. The guitarist already plays, recognizes tonal differences, and owns sounds worth revisiting. They want to direct the work in musical terms and decide its outcome by ear. They have also explicitly enjoyed simultaneous control over several sound characteristics. A one-button preset vending machine would discard the second part of that requirement.

### How this analysis was done

- The project conversation provides first-person descriptions, corrections after listening, and reports of breakdowns. Existing take manifests confirm that one “For the Love of God” recording contains paired dry and wet audio, plus a selected passage after the opening silence and stray note. The conversation supplies the meaning of the equipment and sound choices. This is a retrospective account from one player.
- The evidence register below is a purposive sample of **18 public discussions** across Neural DSP Unity, Line 6, Kemper, and two Reddit guitar communities. Search topics included before/after edits, dry looping, sound versus demonstration, option overload, leveling, and guitar switching. We included posts with a concrete incident, workaround, or dissenting account. Product manuals and original research were read separately. Each forum row represents one discussion, even if several people replied.
- I coded each incident by the player's goal, the variable they wanted to change, what they expected to stay fixed, and the result or workaround. I then tested the design implications against conflicting cases. The method is informed by Contextual Design work models. We have no new in-context observations, interviews, representative sample, or validated accessibility/performance measurements. Forum posts establish that problems and workarounds occur. They cannot establish frequency or willingness to adopt a particular solution. [Beyer and Holtzblatt, Contextual Design](https://chi1995.acm.org/proceedings/tutors/hrb1_bdy.htm)

### The path through a session

1. **Get oriented.** “Which guitar, what is the physical box doing, and what's my starting sound?” Allow an unknown guitar or device state. Keep a record of what was measured, what the player chose, and what the system inferred.
2. **Play or recall a passage.** Start with a previous performance when it is appropriate. Reuse the original processing as a baseline or choose a different one. The selected time window stays fixed through iterations.
3. **Give intent.** A song passage defines a target. A property such as “less bite” changes a direction. “Keep the attack” constrains a change. A favorite preset can supply a starting chain, an audible reference, or the current live sound.
4. **Hear a useful choice.** Move between baseline and candidate at the same point in the phrase, with controlled loudness. A record from a different player or mix is a separately identified listening reference. When investigating feel, play through the change on the hardware.
5. **Act or return to playing.** Keep, reject, or ask for the next change from the sound that was heard. A kept version is recoverable. Writing it to a device, loading it live, and storing a local choice each have a distinct effect.
6. **Resume in another context.** Remember the selected performance, kept sound, open target, and the notes that mattered. A change from quiet home listening to rehearsal updates the use context first.

The important invariant is **stable performance, stable reference, variable sound**. During playing, the most important fact is **which sound is actually active and where it is coming out**. The workstation layout used to ask the player to navigate among a recorder, an advisor, a preset browser, a matching screen, and an independent feedback form to assemble that sequence.

### Tensions to preserve

- **Experimenting can be the musical activity.** R1 includes people who enjoy exploring, and this user explicitly likes the live controls. Show more directions when requested. Make it possible to stop at “good for this song” without grading the entire search space.
- **A fair comparison and a great playing experience differ.** K1 wants the same performance for diagnosis. A player can prefer another tone under their fingers because of dynamics, feedback, or how they change their picking. Offer both checks and keep their observations distinct.
- **Audio matching is underdetermined.** L1 illustrates double-tracking and equipment ambiguity. A tone-only spectral match, matching an audible effect, and copying a physical amp answer different questions. An artist name is a hypothesis. State the chosen part and what evidence supports it.
- **More automation requires clearer control.** Q2 and K3 show that unexplained persistence costs trust. The AI can propose a change or queue work. The player controls what is heard, what replaces the hardware state, and what gets saved. The human-AI research also calls for efficient dismissal, correction, and communicating the consequences of action. [Amershi et al., Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/project/guidelines-for-human-ai-interaction/)
- **The guitar is an instrument first.** A hand motion is already part of making sound. Research prototypes show meaningful possibilities for augmented guitar control, with constraints from occupied hands and performance style. For this user, reliable explicit record/compare controls come before inferred gestures. Compare a pedal or simple keyboard trigger against a calibrated gesture in the same task before assigning anything permanently. [Hödl and Fitzpatrick](https://repositum.tuwien.at/handle/20.500.12708/54699) · [The Stretchy Strap](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/09298215.2023.2274832)

## Interaction concepts considered

| Concept | Where it excels | Where it breaks | Decision |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Voice-first guitar tech | Low physical effort for search and open musical requests. Fits playing posture well in quiet environments. | Speech competes with amplified guitar and can misidentify targets. A transcript of a long exchange hides what sound is active. Room noise, privacy, and accessibility vary. | Use voice as an alternate input to the same actions. Always provide a quick stop and visual playback state. Use explicit activation. |
| Collection of song and sound cards | Easy to browse existing ideas, resume sessions, and use prior decisions as references. | Turns the most frequent action into library maintenance. Artifacts accumulate before the player's first useful sound. | Use a compact saved-sounds picker and persisted session state. |
| Listening desk | After one request, the next decision and its audible evidence sit together. Switching can share a clock. The player can move from choice to action with the guitar still in reach. | A current choice needs strong naming and recovery. A two-sound comparison alone cannot represent complex mixes or a feel test. | **Default surface.** Let third-party reference audio and live tryouts remain distinct. Give details, calibration, and broad search a deliberate entry. |

### The listening desk contract

- **One place to ask.** “Make a Lenny Kravitz sound” proposes a new chain. “A little drier” changes the current draft or playing sound. “Use Plini as the reference” resolves the preset name, asks when it is ambiguous, and leaves the current live sound in place. Any multi-step plan surfaces the next action and names the relevant source.
- **One focal comparison.** A fixed phrase plays through two labeled versions. A/B switches share a cursor for the same recorded performance. External references play as their own excerpts. Default to two meaningful options. Request more when the first difference fails to tell you anything.
- **One way back.** Stop is always reachable. Choosing another take or leaving a preview ends the relevant audio. The current sound, draft, kept sound, and QC save location are legible. After a failure, show which preset is known, whether a recording was retained, and the smallest recovery action.
- **Levels make judgments fair.** Use a common audition loudness with peak headroom. Calibrated input monitoring, output for a gig, and audition attenuation get separate controls. Adopt level discipline as a design principle from subjective audio assessment. This is a workflow comparison, without claims of ITU lab compliance. [ITU-R BS.1116](https://www.itu.int/rec/R-REC-BS.1116-3-201502-I/en)
- **Automation supports the playing.** Persist intent and the exact version heard. Send audio to the local engine. Ask for technical details only when the decision depends on them. Allow a human to examine the chain or use advanced tools without flushing the session.

### Letting Cortex Control stay in the workflow

The player edits on the hardware or in Cortex Control. Tone Lab owns a listening session containing the selected guitar, phrase, reference, draft and exact audition paths. The box's active preset and scene are external observations bearing a “last seen” label. A physical change never changes what an older audition or decision meant. Cortex Control remains the place for detailed chain editing, I/O and bulk preset management. [Cortex Control](https://neuraldsp.com/cortex-control)

| When | Current behavior | Required behavior for seamless integration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cortex Control is open | Tone Lab yields USB device control. It can use saved sounds, make decisions, build drafts, record the four audio channels, and play takes through Windows. For a strong single-input signal pattern it can provisionally identify dry and wet channels and retains the four-channel original. The preset name remains unverified. | Observe stable preset and scene identity through a supported state feed. Verify acquisition provenance without interrupting the editor. |
| The player changes something on the box | Existing comparisons and drafts keep their sources. Returning to Tone Lab requests a fresh observation when the editor is closed. Dirty presets block operations that would recall a different preset. A temporary draft yields if the player selects another preset. | Reconcile state by identity and revision. If the object or parameter changed, show what changed and make retry or rebase explicit. Rollback must apply only to edits still owned by Tone Lab. |
| The Lab must build, audition, or save a new hardware chain | The Lab asks for a short handoff from Cortex Control and keeps the draft ready to retry. It uses dedicated temporary slots and verifies the device result. Selecting **Open Cortex Control** waits for a hardware operation to finish or stops a live preview before reopening the editor. | Build against an isolated working copy and commit through one sequenced controller. Design a handoff for unsaved changes, disconnects, stale versions, and save failures. |

The pinned qc-mcp documentation describes shared HID reads and a light write on CorOS 4.1.0, along with report interleaving and session reset hazards. On this machine and CorOS 4.1.1, testing a shared session while the stock editor was open produced an editor disconnect during an early probe. A later probe joined an existing session and read the firmware, failed to read the complete preset, and the editor exited. Reopening the stock editor with Tone Lab's HID operations parked left it responsive during a silent Windows A/B playback test and a four-channel ASIO capture. These checks establish a working compromise for this installation. They also rule out treating uncoordinated shared HID as the default. [Pinned qc-mcp Windows notes](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/lexasoft123/qc-mcp/f634ecd3dcf426090b90bc4cc176e4b500494b9e/docs/WINDOWS.md)

Two future integration paths are worth evaluating separately. Documented USB MIDI can handle selected performance commands and configured notifications, with explicit mappings and acknowledgements where available. A versioned state feed and single writer for grid edits would handle the larger problem. Before either path becomes default, reproduce editor startup and disconnect races, scene changes during playback, unsaved parameter edits, and interrupted writes. The acceptance test is zero lost player edits and continued access to the exact prior audition after recovery. [Quad Cortex MIDI and USB manual](https://neuraldsp.com/manual/quad-cortex)

### Release and evaluation

| Task to observe with guitar in hand | Proposed success measure | Failure to intentionally test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Start a plausible artist-inspired sound from a cold launch | First meaningful request in one action. A visible next step while the draft is prepared. Player can state what part the draft targets. | Artist-only ambiguity, provider unavailable, no captured take. |
| Compare a real favorite with an existing sound | One named target and audible A/B without touching the pedalboard or changing the playing preset. Player identifies active sound correctly in five switches. | Duplicate preset name, nonstandard routing, front-panel switch during reamp. |
| Compare before and after while listening | Comparable loudness and a continuous cursor on the same take. Measure click-to-audible switch time against an initial goal of under 100 ms once playing. Check that a restart is still available. | Near-silent or clipped version, different performance, audio device missing mid-loop. |
| Play a phrase, refine it, and keep the winner | Capture includes first note, and the original survives. One decision commits a local preference. Writing to the QC is a separate visible action. Observe physical hand-offs and time looking at the screen. | Stop before ready, research timeout, close during test copy, return from sleep. |
| Swap guitar and prepare for rehearsal | Player can recognize which guitar produced the take, retain their home sound, and preserve intentional solo lift. Compare in the actual band mix. | Quiet-room tonal preference differs, guitar/peak levels differ, mono venue feed. |

First run a walkthrough with the existing dry/wet pairs and a readback of the hardware state. Then run the tasks with this guitarist uncoached, including a deliberate refusal of a bad suggestion. Record wrong turns, time to resume playing, unit state confusion, and what the player actually listens for. A useful small follow-up would purposively recruit a player already comfortable making tones, a player overwhelmed by modelers, and a gigging player using the same gear in two rooms. Their different workflows can falsify design assumptions. Results should determine what earns an always-visible control. No claim of player validation is warranted until that happens.
